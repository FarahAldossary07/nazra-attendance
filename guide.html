Start here

What is Nazra?

Nazra is a digital sign-in sheet. Instead of a card, a PIN, or a fingerprint pad, an employee simply looks at a camera and blinks, and the system writes down who they are and the exact time — a clock-in / clock-out record.

What makes it special: it identifies people using only the area around their eyes. Normal face-recognition systems fail when someone wears a niqab because most of the face is covered. Nazra is built for exactly that situation — it never needs to see the nose, mouth, or chin.

The one-sentence version Look at the camera → Nazra recognizes your eyes → you blink to prove you're really there → it logs your name, the date, and the time.

The big picture

Your roadmap — three stages

Getting from zero to a working attendance system has just three stages. You do stage 1 and 2 once; stage 3 is the everyday use.

1

2

3

Everyone looks + blinks to sign in and out. Every day.

Stage 1

What to do first — get it online

The app is already built and saved to your GitHub account. To turn it into a shareable web link, you connect it to a free hosting service called Vercel. This is a one-time, click-through setup — no coding.

  1. Go to vercel.com/new and choose Continue with GitHub (sign in and approve if asked).
  2. Find the project named nazra-attendance in the list and click Import.
  3. Leave the settings as they are — just check: Framework = Other, Build Command = empty, Output Directory = a single dot (.).
  4. Click Deploy and wait about 30 seconds.
  5. You'll receive a link like nazra-attendance.vercel.app. That link is your app. Save it — you'll open it on the computer you want to use as the sign-in station.
Good to know A camera only works on a secure (https) web address — Vercel gives you that automatically, so the link works on any laptop or phone you share it with.

Stage 2

Enrolling your team

"Enrolling" means introducing a person to Nazra so it can recognize them later. You do this once per employee. Open your app link and click ＋ Enroll employee — it opens in a new tab.

  1. Type the person's full name and employee ID (for example, Farah Aldossary · 326003).
  2. Have them look at the camera exactly as they'll appear each day — niqab on. There's no need to uncover.
  3. Click ⚡ Capture 20. While it captures, they slowly turn their head a little and lean in and out, and let the niqab sit slightly differently once or twice.
  4. Aim for about 40 captures total, then click 💾 Save employee.
  5. Repeat for each person. That's it — they're now enrolled.

The next section explains why we take so many pictures, and why we capture in the real covered condition — it's the secret to it working reliably.

The clever part

How it tells two niqab-wearing people apart

This is the question everyone asks: if two people are both covered and you can only see eyes, how can a computer possibly tell them apart? Here's the honest, simple answer.

👁️

Think of it like a fingerprint — but for the eye area

You already recognize your close friends by their eyes alone, even in a mask — the shape of the eye, the eyebrows, the space between them, the little details. The area around the eyes is surprisingly personal. Nazra does the same thing, just with maths instead of intuition.

Here is the whole trick in three steps:

1. CROP Nazra finds your eyes in the camera picture and cuts out just that region (eyes + brows). 2. MEASURE It looks at that eye-region and turns it into a long list of numbers — about 1,280 of them. Think of this list as a "number-fingerprint" of your eyes. Farah's eyes → [0.12, -0.98, 0.34, ... ] Sara's eyes → [0.88, 0.05, -0.21, ... ] Same person's eyes always make a SIMILAR list. A different person's eyes make a DIFFERENT list. 3. COMPARE A live person's number-fingerprint is compared to everyone enrolled. The closest match wins — but only if it's close ENOUGH. If nothing is close enough, Nazra says "UNKNOWN" instead of guessing.
The safety line That "close enough" cut-off is deliberately strict. For an attendance system, logging the wrong person is worse than logging no one — so when Nazra isn't sure, it says UNKNOWN and asks the person to try again, rather than risk a wrong name.

Because two different people produce two clearly different number-fingerprints, Nazra can separate them — even though both are covered and you only ever see their eyes.

Why enrollment matters

Why we take ~40 eye photos (and what they're for)

During enrollment, Nazra isn't just "taking a photo." Each capture is turned into one of those number-fingerprints and stored under the person's name. So 40 captures = 40 reference fingerprints for that person. Here's why more (and varied) is better:

Why many pictures?

A single photo is fragile — tilt your head or change the light and it no longer matches. Many photos, taken as you move slightly, teach Nazra the natural range of how your eyes look. Then a live match just needs to be close to any one of them.

Why capture covered?

Nazra will be used with the niqab on, so we show it that exact look during enrollment — no need to ever uncover. Matching the real day-to-day condition is what makes recognition reliable.

Why move your head?

Turning slightly and leaning in/out spreads the samples across angles and distances — so a person standing a little differently each morning still matches.

What is actually saved?

Only the number-fingerprints and the name + ID — kept privately in that computer's browser. The point of enrollment is simply to build each person's set of reference fingerprints.

In one line Enrollment = teaching Nazra the natural variety of a person's eyes, in the conditions it'll really see them, so everyday recognition is fast and reliable.

The proof-of-life step

Why you blink to log in

After Nazra recognizes someone, it asks them to blink before writing the record. This is a simple anti-cheating check (called "liveness"). A photo of someone's eyes held up to the camera can't blink — a real, present person can. So the blink confirms the right person is actually standing there, not being faked with a picture on a phone.

Nazra detects the blink by watching the eyes briefly close and reopen, then it saves the attendance record with the date and time.

For the curious

The tech stack

Everything runs inside the visitor's own web browser — there is no server watching a camera and nothing is uploaded. Here's what powers it:

🌐

The web page HTML · CSS · JavaScript

Plain web technology — the pages you see and click. No app to install; it opens like any website.

🎥

The camera WebRTC / getUserMedia

The browser's built-in way to access a webcam, with the visitor's permission.

🧠

The eye-and-face AI MediaPipe Tasks for Web

Google's on-device AI. Two parts: a Face Landmarker that locates the eyes and detects blinks, and an Image Embedder that turns the eye-region into the number-fingerprint. Both run locally in the browser via WebAssembly.

💾

The memory Browser localStorage

Enrolled fingerprints and the attendance log are saved privately in the browser on the sign-in computer. Records export to a CSV spreadsheet on demand.

🚀

Hosting GitHub + Vercel

The code lives on GitHub; Vercel serves it to the world as a fast, secure (https) website — and re-publishes automatically whenever the code is updated.

Bonus: there's also an earlier Python desktop version (OpenCV + MediaPipe + Flask) that we used to prove the idea before moving it into the browser.

Under the hood

One honest limitation Because enrolled fingerprints live in one browser, recognition works on the computer where people were enrolled — perfect for a fixed office sign-in station. To recognize the same people across many different devices, the next version would store fingerprints in a shared online database. That's the natural "v2."
